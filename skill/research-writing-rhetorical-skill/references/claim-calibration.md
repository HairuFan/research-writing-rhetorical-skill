# Claim Calibration Rules

Use these rules to align wording with the research design, estimand, evidence, and uncertainty.

## Claim levels

### Level 1 — Description

Reports what was observed without extending beyond the analysis.

Examples: `was observed`, `the sample showed`, `the estimate was`, `the analysis documented`.

### Level 2 — Association

States covariation without causal interpretation.

Examples: `was associated with`, `varied with`, `corresponded to`, `was higher among`.

### Level 3 — Interpretation

Offers a qualified account supported by the pattern.

Examples: `suggests`, `is consistent with`, `supports the interpretation that`, `may indicate`.

### Level 4 — Mechanistic proposition

Proposes a process without claiming definitive identification.

Examples: `may partly explain`, `may operate through`, `appears to contribute to`, `is compatible with a mechanism involving`.

### Level 5 — Causal claim

States that changing one factor changes another.

Examples: `caused`, `produced`, `increased`, `reduced`, `led to`.

Use Level 5 only when the design and identification strategy support it.

## Default design constraints

These are audit defaults, not substitutes for reading the study's identification strategy.

### Cross-sectional observational

- default maximum: Level 2;
- Level 3 may be appropriate for cautious interpretation;
- do not establish temporal order or causality;
- avoid mechanism claims unless supported by additional evidence.

### Longitudinal observational

- default maximum: Level 3;
- temporal ordering may be supported, but confounding can remain;
- do not equate change over time with identified causation.

### Randomized experiment

- Level 5 may be appropriate for the assigned intervention and measured outcome;
- preserve noncompliance, attrition, spillover, measurement, and external-validity limitations;
- do not generalize beyond the experimental population without support.

### Natural or quasi-experiment

- causal language depends on the identification assumptions and diagnostics;
- state the estimand and affected population;
- preserve threats to the design.

### Simulation

Supported claims include:

- behavior under specified data-generating assumptions;
- comparative method performance in the simulated regimes;
- sensitivity to parameter choices.

Unsupported by simulation alone:

- live-deployment effectiveness;
- empirical prevalence;
- population causality;
- universal superiority.

### Qualitative study

Supported claims include:

- participant experiences;
- interpreted processes;
- conceptual mechanisms grounded in the data;
- variation across cases.

Avoid by default:

- population prevalence estimates;
- universal claims;
- causal effects stated as identified facts.

### Systematic review or meta-analysis

- literature-wide claims must follow the search, inclusion, quality, and synthesis procedures;
- absence of included evidence is not automatically evidence of no research;
- heterogeneity and publication bias must remain visible where relevant.

## Common claim errors

### Association written as causation

Before: `Experience causes salary inequality.`

Calibrated: `Salary growth was less strongly associated with experience for women than for men.`

### Nonsignificance written as no effect

Before: `There was no effect.`

Calibrated: `The estimate was imprecise and did not provide clear evidence of an effect.`

### Simulation written as real-world proof

Before: `The framework is effective in deployment.`

Calibrated: `The framework performed consistently across the simulated deployment regimes.`

### Mechanism inferred from a pattern

Before: `Occupational segregation explains the gap.`

Calibrated: `The pattern is consistent with occupational segregation contributing to the gap, although the mechanism is not identified directly.`

### Single setting written as universal

Before: `The method generalizes across organizations.`

Calibrated: `The method was stable across the organizations represented in the evaluation.`

### Descriptive evidence written as policy necessity

Before: `Organizations must retrain the model.`

Calibrated: `The observed disparity may justify additional monitoring or mitigation, depending on uncertainty, sample adequacy, and deployment costs.`

### Unsupported novelty

Before: `This is the first framework to address the problem.`

Calibrated: `The framework contributes by integrating uncertainty, sample adequacy, and decision cost within one intervention rule.`

### Unsupported absence claim

Before: `No studies have examined this issue.`

Calibrated: `The issue remains underdeveloped in the literature reviewed here.`

## Statistical status

Preserve all distinctions among:

- point estimate and interval estimate;
- statistical significance and practical importance;
- absence of evidence and evidence of absence;
- model fit and causal validity;
- subgroup estimate and population conclusion;
- robustness within tested specifications and universal robustness.

Do not delete uncertainty language merely to improve concision.

## Calibration questions

Before finalizing a claim, ask:

1. What design produced the evidence?
2. What estimand or phenomenon was measured?
3. Does the wording imply temporal order, mechanism, causality, prevalence, or generalizability?
4. Does the evidence support that implication?
5. Are uncertainty and boundary conditions preserved?
6. Does a literature-wide or novelty claim require a citation or systematic search?

If the answer to Question 4 is unclear, use a weaker formulation or flag the claim for author review.
